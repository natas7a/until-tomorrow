Part IWeek 1 · Introduction

The big picture

Six steps, one question: given that the future is uncertain, how can we understand change today, explore plausible futures, and make decisions now that remain useful whatever happens?

Think of it like this

The whole fellowship is one long relay race with six runners. Each runner (step) hands the baton to the next, and you can't skip one. Scanning needs Framing to know what to look for. Sensemaking needs Scanning's signals. Scenarios need Sensemaking's system map. Backcasting needs a scenario to walk back from, and Refinement needs a scenario to test. Keep this order in mind: everything below zooms in on one runner at a time.

The six steps, in one line each

  • Framing: determining the focal issue, geographical boundary, time horizon and stakeholders.
  • Scanning: systematically collecting information about trends, driving forces and emerging issues surrounding the focal issue.
  • Sensemaking: analysing and interpreting signals to determine their potential impact on the focal issue and its future.
  • Scenarios: constructing multiple alternative futures through stories about how the focal issue might unfold.
  • Backcasting: planning backward from a desired future to find the path from vision to action.
  • Scenario refinement: revisiting scenarios and analysing whether they are human-centred futures.

Why foresight, and why for child safety?

Most conversations about children and AI react to the latest headline: a new app, a lawsuit, a ban. Foresight asks a different question: not "what will happen?" but "what could happen, and what should we do now that stays useful whichever way things go?"

Children are part of the present generation, but their lives stretch furthest into the future. They will live longest with today's decisions about AI, which makes this a natural topic for futures thinking.

Part IIStep 01 · Week 2

Framing: define the box

Deciding exactly which future you are going to study, and within what boundaries.

In the fellowshipWeek 2 · Framing the issue
What you'll doShare draft focal issues, agree on one for the cohort, map who shapes it and who lives with it
What you'll produceA focal issue card, a stakeholder map and your personal stakeholder lens

What framing is

Framing is the process of deciding exactly what future you are going to study and within what boundaries.

Before you scan for information, you need to know what the investigation is actually about. Otherwise you collect huge amounts of information without knowing whether it matters. "AI and child safety" alone covers AI-generated abuse material, companion chatbots, AI tutors, age verification, children's data and much more. That's far too much to study at once.

Simple analogy

Framing is like setting the zoom and the location pin on a camera before you take a photo. If you don't first decide "I'm photographing this mountain, from here, at sunset", you'll end up with a thousand random, unusable shots. Framing means pointing the camera before you start shooting.

Framing sets four things

What?
Focal issue

The subject or system you are exploring.

Where?
Geographical boundary

Where the exploration applies.

When?
Time horizon

How far into the future you are looking.

Who?
Stakeholders

Who affects the system, or is affected by it.

The method also links framing to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and to a leadership response: which kind of leadership the issue calls for.

Definition from the method

"Determining focal issue, geographical boundary, time horizon, and stakeholders."

Worked example

Good to know

Two ways to phrase a focal issue

  • "The futures of X…": broader. "The futures of children's relationships with conversational AI by 2040."
  • "How might…?": narrower. "How might AI companions shape the way 10-to-15-year-olds form friendships by 2040?"

Both should give you an open-ended question, not a yes/no question.

❌ Yes / no, or a solution in disguise"Should we ban children from AI chatbots?"
→
✅ Open-ended"The futures of children's relationships with conversational AI by 2040"

Choosing the time horizon

The suggested minimum is five years; most foresight work looks 10–20 years ahead. Two things matter most when choosing:

  • The rate of technological change. AI moves very fast, so even 10–15 years is enough to see huge change.
  • Expected socio-political events within your geographical boundary: elections, new laws coming into force, review dates for existing regulation.

Describing the focal issue

Once the issue is chosen, describe it by answering four questions. Acknowledge uncertainties and challenges, and keep your questions open-ended.

What topic are we exploring?

How children use and relate to conversational AI: as a study aid, a confidant, a friend, a toy.

What is its current state?

Use is widespread among teenagers and growing among younger children, while regulation is patchy and differs by country.

Why are its futures important?

These relationships may shape how a generation learns, forms friendships and asks for help.

What desirable outcome do we want to explore?

AI that supports children's development, safety and agency rather than exploiting their attention and trust.

Stakeholders

A stakeholder is anyone who affects, influences or is affected by the issue.

Simple analogy

Think of the focal issue as a pond you're about to drop a stone into. Stakeholders are everyone standing close enough to feel the ripples. Some throw the stone (AI companies, regulators, investors). Others just get splashed (a lonely 12-year-old, a child who doesn't speak a dominant online language, a teacher who finds out afterwards). You need both views, not just the thrower's.

Throw the stone
Shape the system

AI developers, platforms and app stores, toy and edtech companies, investors, national regulators, standards bodies, international organisations (e.g. UNICEF, UN Committee on the Rights of the Child), schools, parents.

Get splashed
Live with the consequences

Children of different ages (under-8s are not teenagers), children in rural areas, children with disabilities, children in care, children who don't speak a dominant online language, families with low digital literacy, teachers, trust & safety workers.

Why this matters

A futures exercise is stronger when you don't look at the system from the perspective of one institution only. An AI companion app might look excellent from the company's engagement dashboard, and completely different from the perspective of a 13-year-old who talks to it every night, a worried parent, a school counsellor or a content moderator.

This is why, in Week 2, each fellow chooses a stakeholder lens to carry through the rest of the programme.

SDGs and children's rights connected to the example

  • SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being (mental health, emotional development)
  • SDG 4: Quality Education (AI in learning, AI literacy)
  • SDG 5: Gender Equality (gendered harms and design choices)
  • SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities (who has access, and who is protected)
  • SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions (target 16.2: ending abuse and exploitation of children)
  • The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, and General Comment No. 25 on children's rights in the digital environment: children's rights to protection, to provision and to participation.

Leadership responses

The anticipatory leadership method pairs framing with a leadership response. For an issue this contested and fast-moving, two fit well: adaptive leadership with a plausible-futures mindset (acting under uncertainty rather than waiting for certainty) and collaborative leadership (no single actor can solve this alone).

Part IIIStep 02 · Week 3

Scanning: collect signals

Looking systematically beyond your immediate field for signals of change that could shape the future.

In the fellowshipWeek 3 · Scanning
What you'll doPitch your signals, sharpen them, check for blind spots with STEEP, map the Futures Triangle
What you'll produceThe Signal Wall, our Futures Triangle and a shortlist of driving forces

What scanning is

Scanning means systematically looking outside the immediate system to find signals of change that could influence the future. You're not just searching for interesting news. You're trying to find out:

  • What is changing?
  • How quickly is it changing?
  • What deeper forces might be causing the change?
  • What emerging developments might become important?
  • What could these changes mean for our focal issue?
Definition from the method

"Collecting information — trends, driving forces, and emerging issues — about the focal issue." Scanning is the "systematic exploration of the external environment".

Simple analogy

Scanning is like being a weather scout on a mountain before a big climb. You're not just noting "it's cloudy today" (a topic or an event). You're watching the wind shift, the pressure drop and the birds behaving oddly, and asking "what does this pattern mean for tomorrow's climb?" That pattern-watching is scanning.

Topic vs. signal

Topics, subjects and events are not automatically useful signals. Foresight scanning looks for signals of change, collective patterns and developments that point towards possible future outcomes. A topic is a container; a signal or pattern is an insight.

❌ A topicArtificial intelligence
→
✅ A signal of changeChildren increasingly turning to AI chatbots for emotional support
The second describes a possible change pattern whose future implications we can actually explore.

The goal of scanning is to understand the nature and pace of change, and to spot opportunities, challenges and likely developments that matter for the focal issue.

Four kinds of signal

Trends

A trend is a pattern of change that develops over time. You're not looking for something that happened once. You're looking for developments that suggest a broader direction of change, for example the rising share of teenagers who have used an AI companion.

Driving forces

A driving force is a deeper structural force that shapes how a system develops. It sits underneath several visible developments at once. A single event is usually too shallow: a driving force should explain why several developments are happening together, and should have effects beyond the near future.

Definition from the method

"Driving forces are the deep structural forces that shape the future. They underlie multiple shallower phenomena, are not one-off events, and have effects not just on the near future."

Simple analogy

A driving force is the tide, not a single wave. One wave crashing (an event, like "a chatbot company is sued") is one visible splash. The tide (the driving force, like "growing legal accountability of AI developers for harms to minors") is what pulls every wave in and out, everywhere, for years. When you notice several waves moving the same way, look for the tide underneath.

❌ EventA lawsuit against a chatbot company
→
✅ Driving forceGrowing legal accountability of AI developers for harms to minors
The first is one manifestation; the second names the broader structural development.
❌ EventAustralia's under-16 social media law
→
✅ Driving forceGrowing state intervention in children's access to digital platforms
❌ Assumes an extreme outcomeEvery child will have an AI best friend
→
✅ Driving forceIncreasing emotional reliance on AI companions among young people
The second is more grounded and doesn't assume where things end up.
❌ Decides the outcome too earlyThe end of privacy for children
→
✅ Driving forceExpanding collection of children's biometric and behavioural data
Rules for a good driving force

It is about a deep force · it has at least some evidence, even weak · it is phrased clearly · it doesn't assume a direction unless that direction is fairly certain.

Emerging issues

An emerging issue is a development that is relatively new, is growing or becoming clearer, and may have a significant future impact. It isn't necessarily a dominant trend yet, which is exactly why it matters in foresight: it may be small now and important later. Example: deepfake images of classmates being used for bullying in schools.

Definition from the method

"New and growing developments that are becoming clearer and could have a big impact in the future."

Weak signals

A weak signal is an early hint that something important may be emerging. It's "weak" because its importance isn't obvious yet. The key foresight skill is not dismissing something just because it's small. Weak signals are connected to emerging issues and should be part of every scan.

Example: a handful of toy makers start selling plush toys with built-in chatbots. At first it looks like a niche product. But if similar products start appearing across toy brands, smart speakers and edtech apps, it could signal a much bigger shift in how young children first meet AI. The point isn't to declare "this will definitely happen". It's to say: "this may be an early sign of a change worth watching."

Trend vs. driving force vs. emerging issue vs. weak signal

Picture a snowball rolling downhill. The weak signal is the first tiny snowflake you barely notice sticking to it. The emerging issue is the snowball once it's visibly growing but still small. The trend is the path it's clearly rolling along. The driving force is gravity itself: the deep, invisible reason all the snow keeps rolling the same way.

Where to scan

Search engines, newspapers, social media, experts, formal and informal groups (including communities of young people and parents), research reports, and reports from futurists. Also try sources outside your own field: that's where surprises tend to come from.

STEEP: five lenses

STEEP divides the external environment into five dimensions, so you don't accidentally scan only the areas you personally find interesting.

Simple analogy

STEEP is like five different coloured lenses on the same pair of binoculars. If you only ever look through the "Technological" lens, you'll miss the new law that just changed what apps can collect, or the business model that makes safety features unprofitable. Switching lenses shows you the whole landscape, not just your favourite corner.

S · Social
Demographics, lifestyles, inclusion, trust

Changing family structures and parenting norms; youth loneliness; declining trust in platforms.

T · Technological
New products, innovation, adoption

AI toys; AI-based age estimation; on-device AI; generative image tools.

E · Economic
Value creation, distribution, competition

Engagement-based business models; cost of compliance for small developers; the edtech market.

E · Environmental
Ecosystems, resources, built spaces, consciousness

Energy and water demand of AI infrastructure; less unstructured outdoor play; climate anxiety online.

P · Political
Laws, regulation, policies, ideologies

Online safety laws; minimum ages; the EU AI Act; geopolitical competition over AI.

The Futures Triangle

The Futures Triangle maps the tension between three forces acting on a focal issue at the same time:

  • Pulls: the images of the future people are drawn towards.
  • Pushes: forces driving change right now.
  • Weights: historical constraints and barriers holding things back.

Instead of asking only "what is changing?", it asks: where does the system want to go, what is pushing it there, and what is holding it back?

Definition from the method

"A strategic foresight tool that maps the dynamic tensions shaping an institution's future." It examines how aspirations (pulls), current forces (pushes) and historical constraints (weights) interact.

Simple analogy

Imagine towing a car up a hill. The pull is the destination at the top. The push is your engine working right now. The weight is a trailer full of old furniture dragging behind you. All three act on the car at once. Foresight asks you to name all three, not just the destination.

Worked example

  • Push (strongest, in this example): children are adopting conversational AI much faster than rules and research can keep up.
  • Pull: competing images. Industry pictures "an AI tutor and friend for every child"; child-rights advocates picture "safe by design by default"; many parents picture "a childhood with fewer screens". Children's own image of the future is often missing.
  • Weight: business models that reward engagement, laws written for older technologies, and the limits of age assurance.
  • Main tension: engagement-driven design vs. children's right to develop free from manipulation.
If someone asks "which force is strongest?"

Don't just name one. Explain why it's strongest, and name the tension between the three.

Part IVStep 03 · Week 4

Sensemaking: connect the signals

Turning a pile of separate signals into a coherent picture of how the system behaves.

In the fellowshipWeek 4 · Sensemaking
What you'll doTurn driving forces into system variables, build a first loop together, map the system in small groups
What you'll produceOur system map, a register of named loops and a note of leverage points

What sensemaking is

Scanning gives you many signals. Sensemaking asks: "What do all these signals actually mean when we put them together?"

You're trying to work out whether something is a one-off event or part of a larger change. You connect signals that look unrelated and look for relationships, causes, feedback and system behaviour.

Definition from the method

"Analysing and interpreting the signals to determine their potential impact on the focal issue." In short: turning ambiguous signals into coherent understanding.

Simple analogy

Scanning puts a pile of puzzle pieces on the table. Sensemaking is turning the pieces over and starting to fit them together. Before the puzzle is finished, you can already guess it's a mountain, not a beach.

Example

Suppose your scan found four separate things: teenagers using AI companions more; parents having limited AI literacy; companion apps earning money from engagement; new online safety laws being passed. Instead of treating them as four unrelated observations, systems thinking asks: how could these influence one another? That question is the start of sensemaking.

Systems thinking

The method uses systems thinking as its main sensemaking approach. Scanning identifies the forces shaping the future. Sensemaking goes deeper, into underlying causes and motivations, and reveals the relationships between variables. A systems map makes hidden assumptions visible and brings out the mental models behind a system.

Causal loop diagrams (CLDs)

A causal loop diagram is a visual map of how variables influence each other, and how those influences can eventually feed back into the system. Instead of "A causes B", you think: A changes B, B changes C, C changes A, and so the system behaves differently over time.

Definition from the method

"A causal loop diagram (CLD) is a map of how variables in a system influence each other and how those influences circle back to produce behaviour over time."

Simple analogy

A CLD is like drawing the plumbing behind a wall instead of just looking at the tap. You already know "turning the tap makes water come out". A CLD shows the pipe from the tap back to the tank and the pump, and how a leak in one pipe eventually changes the pressure everywhere else, including at the tap you started from.

Three components

Nodes
Variables

Anything that can increase or decrease over time.

Links
Causal arrows

An arrow A → B means a change in A causes a change in B.

+ / −
Polarity signs

+ same direction: A up, B up (or A down, B down).
− opposite direction: A up, B down (or A down, B up).

Feedback loops

A feedback loop is a chain of cause and effect that eventually comes back to where it started. The system's output becomes an input that shapes its future behaviour. There are two kinds:

  • Reinforcing loop (R): makes change speed up. It amplifies movement in one direction.
  • Balancing loop (B): makes change settle down, or resists it.
Simple analogy

A reinforcing loop is a snowball rolling downhill: it keeps getting bigger and faster, feeding itself. A balancing loop is a thermostat: when the room gets too hot, the air conditioning kicks in and brings it back down. Same loop shape, opposite behaviour.

The shortcut: count the − signs around the loop

Even number of − signs (including zero) = Reinforcing (R) · Odd number = Balancing (B)

System variables

A system variable is a condition within the system that can become higher or lower over time. The easiest test: can I say "the level of X is increasing / decreasing"? If yes, it's probably a variable.

Definition from the method

"A system variable is any condition that can increase or decrease over time in your system."

Simple analogy

A system variable is a dial or dimmer switch: it has a level that can go up or down ("level of children's trust in AI companions"). An event, like "a new law was passed", is a light switch being flicked: it happened once, it isn't a dial, and you can't say it's "increasing". If you can't picture a slider moving, it probably isn't a variable.

✅ Good variables

Level of children's trust in AI companions

Degree of children's participation in technology design decisions

Level of AI literacy among parents and teachers

❌ Not variables

"OpenAI": an actor

"A new online safety law was passed": an event

"AI": too broad, not a condition that goes up or down

Turning driving forces into system variables

Select the 5–8 driving forces that seem most relevant and impactful (in the fellowship, these are the most-starred signals from the Signal Wall). For each, ask:

  1. Does this driver name a condition in the system, or describe something happening to the system?
  2. If it names a condition, use it or rename it.
  3. If it describes a trend, ask: what state of the system does this produce or affect?
Driving forceIncreasing emotional reliance on AI companions among young people
→
System variableLevel of young people's emotional reliance on AI companions
Driving forceGrowing state intervention in children's access to platforms
→
System variableStrength of legal obligations on platforms towards minors

Clustering and refining variables

Your first list will contain duplicates. Cluster and refine it (the method suggests reducing it to about 15–20; with our session time, 10–12 is plenty). If two variables describe the same underlying condition, merge them and keep the broader wording.

MergeParents' distrust of tech companies + teachers' distrust of edtech providers
→
KeepLevel of trust in technology companies among adults around children
MergeChildren's understanding of how chatbots work + ability to spot AI-generated content
→
KeepLevel of children's AI literacy

Building the system map

  1. Put each variable in a circle or rounded box.
  2. For each pair, ask: does a change in A cause a change in B? If yes, draw A → B. If it works both ways, draw two arrows. Every arrow gets a + or a −.
  3. Follow chains until one comes back to where it started. That's a loop. Count the − signs: even = R, odd = B.
  4. Give each loop a memorable name, e.g. The Engagement Loop, The Trust Drain, The Exclusion Loop.

Your starting variables are not a limit: mapping relationships often reveals a missing variable that needs to be added.

What the map should help you see

Which drivers are most influential?

The variables with the most arrows going in and out are possible leverage points.

What is the system optimising for?

In our example, the map might suggest it optimises for engagement and growth, not for children's development. Is that what children need?

Where are the tensions?

E.g. between protecting children and respecting their right to participate; between access and safety; between regions with very different rules.

Link to the Week 4 deep dive

"Level of children's AI literacy" is a good variable to practise on. What increases it? What does more of it change? And a harder question: is AI literacy a protective factor, or does it shift responsibility for safety onto children themselves? Mapping it as a loop helps you see both answers.

Part VStep 04 · Week 5

Scenarios: four possible worlds

Building several plausible futures, so you aren't caught out by any of them.

In the fellowshipWeek 5 · Scenarios
What you'll doChoose two critical uncertainties, build a 2×2 matrix, write day-in-the-life stories through your stakeholder lens
What you'll produceFour named scenarios with narratives, and a vote on which one to backcast from

What a scenario is (and isn't)

A scenario is a plausible description of a possible future. It is not a prediction. It is not a desired future. It is not a policy recommendation.

It's a way of asking: "If the world developed under these particular conditions, what would that world look like?"

Definition from the method

"Constructing multiple alternative futures through stories about how our focal issues might unfold over time."

Simple analogy

A scenario is like packing for four different kinds of weather before a trip. You're not predicting rain. You're preparing for "what if it rains?" as well as "what if it's sunny, snowy or windy?" You explore all four so you're not caught off guard by any of them.

The 2×2 scenario matrix

The 2×2 matrix takes two major uncertainties and crosses their two possible extremes. This gives you four different plausible futures. The point isn't to decide which is good or bad. It's to explore different operating environments: different worlds in which children, families, companies and governments would have to act.

Definition from the method

The tool selects two critical uncertainties: factors that strongly shape outcomes but whose future direction cannot be confidently predicted. Crossing them produces four distinct scenarios.

Choosing critical uncertainties

  • High impact: it would change the focal issue a lot.
  • High uncertainty: we genuinely can't tell which way it will go.
  • Independent: all four combinations can exist. If one axis automatically drives the other, choose a different pair.
  • About the world, not our strategy: "how much public trust there is in AI" is an uncertainty; "whether we run awareness campaigns" is a strategy.

Things that are almost certain (for example, "children will grow up surrounded by AI in some form") aren't axes. They're predetermined elements, and they appear in all four scenarios.

The two uncertainties in our example

Axis 1 · horizontal
Who sets the rules for AI used by children?

Self-regulation: companies set their own rules, with voluntary codes and uneven enforcement.

Binding public rules: enforceable, internationally coordinated obligations with independent oversight.

Axis 2 · vertical
What is children's role in shaping AI?

Passive users: decisions are made for children by adults, companies and states.

Active co-designers: children and young people are formally involved in design, testing, audits and rule-making.

Writing scenarios

For each quadrant, describe the overall context, the key conditions, and what is normal in that future for children, families, companies and governments.

❌ That's a strategy"Governments should require child-rights impact assessments…"
→
✅ That's a scenario"In this future, governments require child-rights impact assessments…"
A scenario describes the world. It doesn't say what you think should be done.

Scenario narratives: a day in the life

A scenario is much easier to understand as a day-in-the-life story. Choose a persona. In the fellowship, use your stakeholder lens: a 9-year-old with an AI toy, a 15-year-old who confides in a chatbot, a parent, a teacher, a trust & safety moderator, a regulator.

Then describe one specific day and one decision or moment: what the person sees, does and struggles with. Make it concrete and sensory rather than repeating bullet points. Aim for 150–250 words and give the scenario an evocative, memorable title.

Example · Safe but Silent, a Tuesday in 2040

Lina is 14 and needs help with a chemistry project. The school's approved AI tutor greets her by name, and then refuses her question: "compounds" is on its restricted list for under-16s. To unlock the full version she needs a parent's digital ID and a face scan, and her dad is on a night shift. Her friend Omar sends her a link to an unofficial chatbot "that doesn't ask questions". It answers everything, instantly, including things nobody asked it. Lina closes it, uneasy, and goes back to the approved tutor, which suggests she "ask a trusted adult". She types her question into a search bar instead. At dinner, her mum mentions a news story: harm reports from AI tools are at a record low. Lina thinks about Omar's link and says nothing.

What the example teaches

In this illustrative matrix, a key insight is that strong rules alone don't guarantee good outcomes. "Safe but Silent" has binding rules but leaves children out, so protection comes at the cost of access and agency, and risk moves somewhere less visible. Meanwhile "The Youth Panel Economy" shows that participation without accountability is fragile.

As in the original ASEFYLS exercise, the lesson is that the pieces need to develop together: in our case, rules, children's participation and technology design.

Part VIStep 05 · Week 6

Backcasting: walk back to today

Starting from a desired future and working backwards to what needs to happen now.

In the fellowshipWeek 6 · Backcasting & refinement
What you'll doDefine success for a decision-maker, build timelines back to today, sort moves into no-regret and contingent, name signposts
What you'll produceBackcasting timelines, a strategy table with signposts, and the basis for your Week 7 position paper

What backcasting is

Backcasting is one of the most important ideas in the whole method.

Imagine your desired future has already happened. Instead of asking "what will probably happen?", you ask: "If we successfully reached this future, what would have needed to happen just before it? And before that? And before that?" You keep moving back until you reach today. Those backward steps then become possible strategic actions.

Definition from the method

"Planning backward from a desired future to determine the path from vision to action."

Simple analogy

Backcasting is like planning a road trip in reverse. You know you want to arrive in a new city by Sunday night. So you ask: "Where must I be on Saturday night? And Friday? And where do I need to leave from today?" You work back from the destination, one stop at a time, until the first stop is right where you're standing.

The logic, in one chain

In 2040, children help set and check the rules for AI they use. ↓ Before that, child-rights impact assessments with youth involvement must be legally required. ↓ Before that, we need agreed design standards, trained young auditors and solid evidence. ↓ Before that, we need a coalition of young people and child-rights organisations working together today. That's backcasting.

How to backcast, step by step

Step 1
Choose a decision-maker

A youth organisation, a regulator, an AI company's trust & safety team, a school network, an international body.

Step 2
Choose one scenario

Preferably the most desirable one (in the fellowship, the one the cohort voted for in Week 5).

Step 3
Define success

"In this scenario, what would success look like for us in 2040?"

Step 4
Walk backwards

Name the conditions and milestones needed just before success, then keep going back to the present.

Step 5
Identify strategic moves

Turn each milestone into something the decision-maker can actually do.

Worked example

Decision-maker: a youth-led organisation working on children's digital rights. Chosen scenario: The Children's Charter (binding rules + children as co-designers).

Success in 2040: children and young people hold formal seats on AI standards and audit bodies; every AI product aimed at children goes through an independent child-rights impact assessment; children have accessible ways to report problems and get redress; and youth-led organisations are recognised as partners rather than consulted once and forgotten.

Milestones: what must be true by then?
Strategic moves: what can the decision-maker do?
Presentnow

Rules are patchy and mostly voluntary; children are rarely consulted; there's little data on how younger children use AI.

Map existing youth advisory mechanisms and design codes. Document how children actually use AI. Build a coalition with other youth and child-rights organisations.

Short term2027–2030

Child-rights design standards agreed; first youth-involved audits piloted; evidence base established.

Co-write standards with children. Train young auditors. Pilot audits with willing companies. Advocate for impact-assessment requirements.

Medium term2031–2035

Impact assessments legally required in several regions; youth seats on national and international standards bodies.

Campaign for youth seats. Build an international network of youth auditors. Support regulators in setting up children's panels.

Long term2036–2040

Children co-govern AI rules across regions; participation is routine, funded and renewed.

Keep participation diverse and renew it with each new generation. Monitor whether it actually changes outcomes for children.

Robust and contingent strategies

Robust strategies, or "no-regret moves"

Some strategies stay useful even if the future turns out differently from the scenario you planned for. They're especially valuable because you don't need to know which future will happen before acting. Robust / no-regret = useful across multiple futures.

  • Build AI literacy for children, parents and teachers.
  • Create permanent (not one-off) youth advisory mechanisms.
  • Improve data on how children actually use AI, broken down by age and region.
  • Build lasting partnerships between child-rights organisations, researchers and developers.
Robust vs. contingent

A no-regret move is like an umbrella that also works as a sunshade: useful whether it rains or shines, so you always bring it. A contingent strategy is like a raincoat you only put on once you see clouds forming: you wait for the signal before acting, because wearing it too early would be pointless if it stays sunny.

Contingent strategies

A contingent strategy only becomes appropriate when a particular future starts to unfold, so you watch for signs that tell you when to switch it on.

Definition from the method

"Some actions only make sense if one scenario starts to unfold. These can become contingent strategies."

  • If "Engagement Wins" starts to unfold: set up independent ratings of AI products for children and public watchdog reporting.
  • If "Safe but Silent" starts to unfold: campaign for children's rights to participation and access, and test age-appropriate access models.
  • If "The Children's Charter" starts to unfold: move fast to secure youth seats on the new bodies before their membership is fixed.
Easy distinction

No-regret: "do it anyway." · Contingent: "do it if the relevant future starts to appear."

Signposts

A signpost is an observable signal that a particular future may be becoming more likely. It lets you watch the environment instead of waiting until a future has already arrived.

Definition from the method

"Signposts are signals that tell us which future may be unfolding."

Simple analogy

A signpost is like watching the sky for storm clouds before deciding whether to grab that raincoat (your contingent strategy). Clouds don't guarantee rain, but they're evidence worth watching. Once you spot enough of them, you know it's time to act.

Signposts to watch in our example:

  • New laws aimed specifically at AI companions or AI toys.
  • Regulator inquiries into chatbots and children.
  • Companies publishing child-rights impact assessments, or pulling products after safety reports.
  • Standards bodies opening seats to young people.
  • Survey data on children's use of, and reliance on, AI.
  • Court rulings on developers' liability for harms to minors.
  • Platforms moving away from engagement-based revenue for products used by teenagers.
How the three fit together

Scenario = a possible future. Signpost = evidence that this future may be emerging. Contingent strategy = the action you switch on when that evidence becomes strong enough.

Part VIIStep 06 · Week 6

Scenario refinement: look behind the scenery

Checking what would actually have to exist for a future to work.

What refinement is

Scenario refinement means going back into a scenario and asking: "What would actually have to exist for this world to work?" This stops you from creating attractive but shallow scenarios. You look at technology, infrastructure, data, skills, institutions, resources, ownership, operation, regulation and maintenance.

Questions from the method

What technologies are explicitly present? What technologies are implicitly assumed? What infrastructure, data, skills, institutions and resources are needed? Who develops, owns, operates, regulates or maintains them? What changes if those technologies are unavailable, unreliable or unaffordable?

Simple analogy

If a scenario is a film set, refinement is walking behind the painted backdrop to check it isn't just flat cardboard. Is there real wiring, plumbing and staff behind "The Children's Charter" (auditors, funding, age-appropriate reporting tools, translated materials), or does it only look good from the front row?

Five roles a technology can play

For any technology in a scenario, ask five questions. Here they are applied to an AI companion app:

Enabler

What becomes possible because of it?

Homework help at any hour, language practice, company for isolated children, accessibility support for children with disabilities.

Disruptor

What existing practice, institution or livelihood does it change?

How friendships form, who children turn to for advice, the role of parents, school counsellors and helplines.

Gatekeeper

Who must access or understand it to take part?

Children without devices or AI literacy are left out; age-assurance tools decide who gets in at all.

Risk source

What harm, dependency or vulnerability could it create?

Emotional dependence, manipulation, inappropriate content, large-scale collection of children's intimate data.

Power amplifier

Whose authority, influence or control does it strengthen?

The companies that own the models and the data, and potentially any state able to access them. Technology is never politically neutral.

All five roles at once

Think of a smartphone in a classroom. It enables access to any lesson online. It disrupts the traditional role of the textbook. It's a gatekeeper, because children without one are left out. It's a risk source if it exposes children to misinformation or scams. And it's a power amplifier for whichever company or government controls the app store and the platform rules. One object, five different questions.

The five roles across our four scenarios

Scenario 1 · The Youth Panel Economy

Co-design tools: enabler. Company-controlled panels: power amplifier for the firms that run them.

Scenario 2 · The Children's Charter

Independent audit systems and child-friendly reporting tools: enabler. Compliance costs: gatekeeper for small developers.

Scenario 3 · Engagement Wins

Engagement-optimised companions: risk source and power amplifier. Paid parental-control tools: gatekeeper (protection depends on income).

Scenario 4 · Safe but Silent

Identity and age checks: gatekeeper and power amplifier (for whoever holds the ID data). Unofficial chatbots: risk source.

Part VIIIRefinement, continued · Week 6

Human-centred futures: who does it work for?

The key question changes from "does this future work?" to "does this future work for people, and for which people?"

For each scenario, the method asks you to assess: what works, what concerns us, who is affected, and what must change. In our fellowship, "people" means above all children, in all their diversity.

Simple analogy

A scenario can look flawless in a spreadsheet (record-low harm reports, high digital access) and still fail real people. Think of a beautiful new motorway that works perfectly for cars while cutting straight through a neighbourhood's only park. Human-centred thinking is the step where you ask: "Who actually has to live in this future?"

Six human-centred dimensions

1 · Rights & accountability

Are rights protected, and can powerful actors be held responsible?

Can a child, or their family, actually complain and get a result when an AI product harms them?

2 · Dignity & well-being

Can people live secure, healthy and meaningful lives?

A future shouldn't be judged only by efficiency or growth. Does it protect children's mental health, sleep, play and privacy?

3 · Agency & participation

Can people shape decisions and the direction of this future?

Are children treated as more than recipients of systems designed by governments, institutions or companies?

4 · Equity & inclusion

Who benefits? Who bears the burdens? Who may be left behind?

Think about age, gender, disability, language, income and region.

5 · Capability & development

Can people develop, contribute, and keep meaningful relationships and identities?

The question isn't only whether children have access to AI, but whether they actually grow: learning, forming friendships, building independence.

6 · Inclusion & belonging

Does this future sustain relationships, solidarity and mutual responsibility?

Especially important where AI access increases while human connection, participation or belonging may decline.

Worked example: "Engagement Wins"

What works

Cheap, widely available tools; fast innovation; some genuinely helpful learning and accessibility features.

What concerns us

Emotional dependence on companions; manipulation for engagement; weak protection and little redress; data harvested from children.

Who is affected

Companies and digitally confident, well-off families benefit most. Children with less parental support, lower digital literacy or in poorer regions carry most of the risk.

What must change

Enforceable baseline protections, independent oversight, and children's voices in how products are designed.

Part XIReady-made answers

If you're put on the spot

Short answers you can say almost word for word if your mind goes blank in a session or in the simulation. Tap a question to open it.

What is the point of scanning?

"Scanning helps us understand the nature and pace of change by systematically looking for trends, driving forces, emerging issues and weak signals around our focal issue. The key point is that foresight scanning looks for signals of change and collective patterns, not isolated facts."

What is a driving force?

"A driving force is a deeper structural force shaping the future. It sits underneath several phenomena, isn't a one-off event, and should be phrased without assuming an uncertain outcome, like 'growing legal accountability of AI developers for harms to minors'."

What is sensemaking?

"Sensemaking is where we move from collecting signals to interpreting them. We ask whether individual signals are part of larger changes, and connect them using systems thinking, variables and causal relationships."

What is a causal loop?

"It's a circular chain of cause and effect where an output feeds back into the system as an input. A reinforcing loop speeds change up; a balancing loop slows it down or resists it."

What is backcasting?

"Backcasting starts with a desired future and works backwards to find the conditions and milestones needed to reach it. Those backward steps then become strategies."

What is a no-regret move?

"It's an action that stays useful across several plausible futures, so we can do it now even though we don't know which future will happen. Building children's and parents' AI literacy is a good example."

What is a signpost?

"A signpost is an observable signal that helps us tell which future may be unfolding, so we can monitor developments and switch on contingent strategies at the right time."

Why human-centred futures?

"Because a future can't be judged only by whether it works technically. We also need to ask who benefits, who bears the burdens, whether rights are protected, whether children have agency, whether they can develop, and whether the future sustains inclusion and belonging."
